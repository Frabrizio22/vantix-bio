Net Peptide Content: Why a 10 mg Vial Can Hold Only 8 mg of Peptide

A vial labeled 10 mg can contain 8 mg of actual peptide and still be 99% pure. Here is why, how to check it, and what a label should tell you.

VB
Vantix Bio Research TeamPublished Oct 8, 2026 · Updated Oct 8, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Net peptide content (NPC) is the share of the powder's weight that is peptide. The rest is counter-ions, water and residual salts or solvents.
  • Purity and net content are different numbers. A 99% pure peptide can still weigh out at 80% peptide, so a "10 mg" vial holds about 8 mg.
  • The gap changes both your working concentration and your cost per milligram of peptide. A label should be close to what is actually in the vial.
  • Ask any vendor for the measured net content of your batch, the method used, and the test date.

Most buyers compare peptides by the number on the label and the purity on the COA. Neither tells you how much peptide is actually in the vial. That is a separate measurement called net peptide content, and it is the one that decides what you really have to work with.

What Net Peptide Content Means

A lyophilized peptide vial does not contain only peptide. The white powder is a mixture of the peptide itself, the counter-ions it is bound to, and water. Net peptide content is the percentage of that powder's weight that is peptide [1][2].

Here is an illustrative example of a 10 mg powder (not Vantix Bio data):

What a "10 mg" powder can contain (illustrative)

Peptide materialabout 8.0 mg (80% net peptide content)
Counter-ions (TFA or acetate)about 1.2 mg
Waterabout 0.7 mg
Other salts and residual solventsabout 0.1 mg

Net peptide content counts all peptide material, including closely related peptide impurities [1]. So the amount of the target peptide is net content multiplied by purity. At 80% net content and 99% purity, about 79% of the powder is the target peptide, or roughly 7.9 mg of the 10 mg.

Why the Gap Exists

Three things take up weight that is not peptide:

  • Counter-ions. Synthetic peptides are usually purified by reversed-phase HPLC with TFA in the mobile phase, so they are typically delivered as TFA salts. The counter-ion pairs with positively charged groups: the N-terminus and the side chains of lysine, histidine and arginine [1][2]. A peptide with many basic residues carries more counter-ion weight, so its net content is lower.
  • Water. Lyophilized peptides are often hygroscopic and can hold a considerable amount of bound water, especially hydrophilic sequences. The amount depends on the sequence, the freeze-drying process and how the vial has been stored [1][2].
  • Residual salts and solvents. Small amounts of process salts and solvents can remain after purification.

Because these depend on the sequence and on each production run, net content differs between peptides and can differ between batches of the same peptide. That is one more reason a batch-level report matters. See why most peptide COAs don't mean what you think.

Purity vs. Net Content

These are two different questions, answered by two different measurements. A vial can score high on one and low on the other.

MeasurementWhat it tells youWhat it ignores
HPLC purityHow much of the detected peptide material is the target peptideWater, counter-ions and salts, because they do not absorb UV light [1]
Net peptide contentHow much of the powder's weight is peptideWhether that peptide is the right one or how pure it is
LC-MS identityWhether the main species has the expected molecular massHow much of it is in the vial

Purity alone can mislead. Read Peptide Purity Explained for how HPLC purity works, and What Your COA Isn't Telling You for the other gaps a purity number leaves open.

What It Means for Concentration and Cost

Concentration. If a stock solution is calculated as though all 10 mg of powder were peptide, but the powder is 80% peptide, the real amount of peptide is 8 mg, which is 20% lower than intended. To get a target amount of peptide, weigh the powder like this:

Powder to weigh = target peptide mass ÷ net peptide content. To get 10 mg of peptide from a powder with 80% net content, weigh 10 ÷ 0.80 = 12.5 mg of powder.

Cost. Net content also changes what you are paying per milligram of peptide. An illustrative example for a $40 vial labeled 10 mg:

Net peptide contentPeptide in the vialPrice per mg of peptide
100%10.0 mg$4.00
90%9.0 mg$4.44
80%8.0 mg$5.00
70%7.0 mg$5.71

A vial that looks cheaper per labeled milligram can cost more per milligram of peptide. Comparing vendors fairly means comparing measured content, not the label.

How Net Content Is Measured

HPLC purity cannot measure net content, so labs use other methods:

  • Amino acid analysis: the peptide is broken down into its amino acids, which are quantified against standards, giving the amount of peptide present [1].
  • Elemental (nitrogen) analysis: measures nitrogen, which comes almost entirely from the peptide, as an indicator of peptide content [1][2].
  • Water and counter-ion measurements: Karl Fischer titration for water, and ion chromatography or titration for counter-ions, account for the non-peptide weight [2].

Some labs also use other quantitative methods, such as quantitative HPLC against a reference standard or qNMR. Every method has measurement uncertainty, so a COA that gives a content figure should also say how it was measured.

Should Net Content Match the Label?

We think the label should be close to what is actually in the vial. There are two honest ways to label a peptide vial: by the amount of peptide, or by the weight of the powder with the net content disclosed next to it. The problem is a label that reads like an amount of peptide but is really the powder weight, with a 20 to 30% gap nobody mentions.

As a rule of thumb (not an industry standard), here is how to read the gap between the label and the measured peptide content on a COA:

Measured vs. labelHow to read it
Within a few percent (including slightly above)Normal. A result like 10.1 mg on a 10 mg label is within measurement uncertainty.
About 10% belowWorth asking about. Could be sequence-dependent water or counter-ion weight.
20% or more belowThe label is probably the powder weight. Ask whether the label means peptide or powder, and for the measured figure.

What to Ask Any Vendor

  1. Does the COA report measured net peptide content for my batch? A purity percentage alone does not answer this.
  2. Does the label state peptide or powder weight? If powder, what is the net content?
  3. How was it measured? A figure with no method is hard to trust.
  4. Is the result tied to my batch ID and a test date? Content varies by batch, so a result for another batch does not describe yours. See 5 red flags on fake COAs.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is net peptide content?

Net peptide content is the percentage of a peptide powder's weight that is actually peptide. The rest is counter-ions (usually TFA or acetate), bound water, and residual salts or solvents.

Is net peptide content the same as purity?

No. Purity is measured by HPLC and compares the target peptide with other peptide-related material; it ignores water and salts. Net content measures how much of the powder's weight is peptide. A peptide can be 99% pure and still have a net content well below 100%.

Why does my vial hold less peptide than the label says?

Usually because the label reflects the weight of the powder, which includes counter-ions and water. The more basic residues a peptide has and the more water it holds, the lower its net content. The COA's measured content shows what the batch actually contains.

How do I calculate how much powder to weigh for a target amount of peptide?

Divide the target peptide mass by the net peptide content as a fraction. To get 10 mg of peptide from a powder with 80% net content, weigh 12.5 mg of powder.

Does a lower net content mean a worse peptide?

Not by itself. Net content depends on the sequence, the counter-ion and the water content, so it differs between peptides. What matters is that it is measured, reported for your batch, and accounted for when you calculate concentrations.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does >99% peptide purity mean?

Purity is the percentage of the target peptide relative to the other peptide-related material HPLC detects. At 99%, about 1% of the detected peak area is other peptide-related species such as truncated sequences or by-products. Counter-ions and water are not counted; they affect net peptide content instead.

How is peptide purity measured?

By HPLC (High-Performance Liquid Chromatography). The sample is dissolved in solvent, passed through a column under high pressure, and components are separated and detected with UV light. Purity = (target peptide peak area / total peak areas) x 100.

Why does third-party testing matter for peptide purity?

Independent testing reduces the conflict of interest, because the lab is not the party selling the product and its credibility depends on accurate reports. Manufacturer-only testing creates a conflict because the producer profits from passing results. Since vendors usually pay the lab, check that a report can be verified with the lab itself.

What's the difference between purity and content?

Purity is what percentage of the peptide material is the correct sequence (99% is very pure). Content is how many milligrams of peptide material are in the vial (30.09 mg versus 30 mg labeled). A vial can have high purity but low content, or the reverse.
